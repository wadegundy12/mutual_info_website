01

Dependence, measured in bits.

Mutual information compares the world you observe to a world where the two variables are independent.

I(X;Y) = DKL(p(x,y) ‖ p(x)p(y))
= Σx,y p(x,y) log₂ [p(x,y) / (p(x)p(y))]

The joint distribution p(x,y) describes which pairs occur. The product p(x)p(y) describes the pairs expected under independence. MI is the KL divergence between them.

Symmetric: swapping X and Y leaves the expression unchanged. This does not reverse the two arguments of KL.

Nonnegative: KL is always ≥ 0. MI is zero exactly when the joint equals the product of the marginals: independence.

Individual terms in the sum may be negative. Their weighted total cannot be. Statistical dependence does not establish causation.

Try this: set the flip probability to 0.5. Then try 1. A bit that is always wrong is still perfectly informative—you can invert it.

02

Information is uncertainty removed.

Entropy asks how uncertain X is. Conditional entropy asks how uncertain X remains after observing Y, averaged over Y.

I(X;Y) = H(X) − H(X|Y)
= H(Y) − H(Y|X)
= H(X) + H(Y) − H(X,Y)

The overlap in an entropy Venn diagram represents shared information. The X-only region is H(X|Y), the Y-only region is H(Y|X), and the union is H(X,Y).

For a fair bit copied correctly 75% of the time, H(X) = 1 and H(X|Y) = h₂(0.25) ≈ 0.811. Observing Y removes 0.189 bits on average.

h₂(t) = −t log₂ t − (1−t) log₂(1−t)
Use 0 log₂ 0 = 0 at the endpoints.

The two-variable Venn picture is a mnemonic, not a universal geometry. For three variables some information-diagram regions can be negative. Conditional entropy is an average; a particular observation can increase uncertainty.

Where does the identity come from?

Expand the log ratio into log p(x,y) − log p(x) − log p(y). The joint term sums to −H(X,Y); the two marginal terms sum to H(X) and H(Y).

03

What does the next variable add?

Conditional MI measures the additional information from Y once Z is already known. Conditioning can remove dependence—or reveal it.

I(X;Y|Z) = H(X|Z) − H(X|Y,Z)
I(X;Y,Z) = I(X;Z) + I(X;Y|Z)

Compute MI within each value of Z, then average those values using p(z). The result is nonnegative and equals zero exactly at conditional independence (for Z values with positive probability).

XOR: X and Y are independent fair bits, and Z is their exclusive OR. Without Z, Y tells you nothing about X. Once Z is known, Y determines X.

Duplicate: X = Y = Z is a fair bit. Y is informative on its own, but adds nothing once Z is known.

Try this: compare the two examples, then condition on Z = 0 or Z = 1. Conditioning is not simply “subtracting off correlation.”

04

Look beyond straight lines.

Pearson correlation sees linear association. MI can detect curves, waves, and other forms of dependence—but estimating it from finite data takes care.

For X uniform on {−1, 0, 1} and Y = X², Pearson correlation is zero. Yet X determines Y exactly, so I(X;Y) = H(Y) ≈ 0.918 bits.

MI has no positive or negative sign. A strongly decreasing relationship can be just as informative as a strongly increasing one.

Jointly Gaussian X, Y:
I(X;Y) = −½ log₂(1−ρ²)

This formula requires joint Gaussianity. As |ρ| approaches 1, continuous MI diverges. Zero correlation does not generally imply independence.

How do we estimate MI?

Histograms: divide the scatterplot into bins and use cell frequencies. Bin choices affect the answer, and fine grids tend to inflate empirical MI.

KSG: the Kraskov–Stögbauer–Grassberger estimator uses nearest-neighbor distances in the joint space, together with marginal neighbor counts. It avoids a fixed grid, but still depends on sample size, the choice of k, and dimensionality. The plot here uses a histogram estimator, not KSG.

Try this: choose “Parabola,” then “Independent.” Both can have correlation near zero. Increase the bins on independent data to see estimation bias.

05

One shared scale. A careful denominator.

NMI(X,Y) = I(X;Y) / min(H(X), H(Y))

For discrete variables with positive finite entropies, MI cannot exceed either entropy. This book's normalization therefore lies in [0, 1].

0 means independence. 1 means the lower-entropy variable is fully determined by the other. It does not require the variables to determine each other.

In the X² example, NMI is 1 even though Y does not tell you the sign of X. If one variable is constant, the denominator is zero and this ratio is undefined.

Continuous differential entropy can be negative and changes with measurement units. MI is invariant under invertible coordinate changes, so replacing H with differential entropy does not preserve this 0–1 normalization.

06

A grid can uncover a pattern.

MIC searches across two-dimensional grid sizes and boundary placements for a high normalized mutual information score.

Grid score = I(column; row) / log₂ min(r,c)
MIC: maximize over permitted grids

A grid with r columns and c rows turns coordinates into discrete categories. MIC searches for a useful partition under a sample-size-dependent cell budget. Its denominator is the maximum possible entropy for the smaller axis—not the empirical entropy used by the NMI above.

Equitability aims to assign similar scores to equally noisy relationships of different shapes. The definition of noise matters; it is not an unrestricted guarantee.

StatisticPrimary goal
MICeQuantify relationship strength equitably
TICeScreen for dependence with high statistical power

MICe is an efficiently computable consistent estimator of a population MIC measure. TICe aggregates information across grids for independence testing.

07

Processing cannot invent information.

X → Y → Z implies I(X;Z) ≤ I(X;Y)

For this Markov chain, Z is produced from Y without additional access to X. Once Y is known, Z and X are conditionally independent.

Here X is a fair source bit. The first channel flips it with probability a; the second independently flips Y with probability b. The effective flip rate from X to Z is a + b − 2ab.

Preprocessing can still improve a model by making information easier to use. The inequality limits information in the data, not what a particular model can learn from it.

A short chain-rule proof

I(X;Y,Z) = I(X;Y) + I(X;Z|Y) = I(X;Y), because the last term is zero for this chain.

In the other order, I(X;Y,Z) = I(X;Z) + I(X;Y|Z). The final term is nonnegative, so I(X;Z) ≤ I(X;Y).

Try this: set the second flip rate to 1. Deterministic inversion preserves information. Then set it to 0.5: all source information disappears.

08

Keep the evidence. Discard the detail.

A sufficient statistic preserves all information relevant to a parameter—not necessarily everything about the original data.

T(D) is sufficient for θ if the conditional distribution of data D given T(D) no longer depends on θ. Once the summary is known, the remaining details add no evidence about θ.

Bernoulli: with fixed known sample size n, the success count S = Σxᵢ is sufficient for θ, because the likelihood depends on the data only through S.

L(θ;D) = θS(1−θ)n−S

Gaussian: for independent N(μ,σ²) samples with known σ² and fixed n, the sample mean is sufficient for μ. Unknown variance requires an additional statistic measuring spread.

Minimal sufficient means the coarsest summary that remains sufficient: it can be computed from every other sufficient statistic. The count and the mean above are minimal sufficient in their respective models.

In a Bayesian formulation with a random parameter, sufficiency gives I(θ;D) = I(θ;T(D)). It is a lossless summary specifically for learning θ.

09

Some prediction errors are unavoidable.

Fano's inequality turns uncertainty about a class label into a lower bound on classification error.

H(Y|X) ≤ h₂(Pₑ) + Pₑ log₂(K−1)
Pₑ ≥ max(0, [H(Y|X)−1] / log₂ K)

Y is a label with K classes. X is the available feature set. Pₑ is the error probability of a classifier using those features. The second expression is a simpler, weaker bound.

Because H(Y|X) = H(Y) − I(X;Y), informative features reduce the remaining uncertainty. This motivates selecting a feature set with high joint MI with the label.

A candidate feature's added information is I(Y;Xⱼ|XS). This avoids rewarding duplicates, but greedy selection can still miss combinations such as XOR.

Try this: compare “Random guessing” with “Half a bit of information” in the binary case. The simplified bound can be zero even when the tighter bound is informative.
How is the tighter bound computed?

For the Bayes-optimal classifier, error is at most 1−1/K. On [0, 1−1/K], h₂(e) + e log₂(K−1) increases from 0 to log₂ K. We numerically invert this function at H(Y|X). The resulting lower bound also applies to every classifier, because none can beat Bayes error.

A lower bound is not an error prediction. High MI does not guarantee that a limited model or training procedure will achieve low error.
